# Twenty-four libraries, one unsolved chart problem

No library in the current open-source JavaScript charting landscape wins on architecture, performance, bundle size, ergonomics, accessibility, and licensing simultaneously — every one of the 24 libraries examined trades at least two of these against each other, and the trades cluster into a small number of predictable, well-documented fault lines. Rendering technology (SVG vs. Canvas vs. WebGL) is the single biggest lever: it predetermines a library's performance ceiling, its accessibility defaults, and its ability to server-render, so libraries don't choose these properties independently — they inherit them from an architecture decision made at birth. Licensing has quietly become a second major fault line as "open source" darlings like ApexCharts move to revenue-gated dual licenses and Highcharts/amCharts/LightningChart remain source-viewable-but-commercial, while governance quality diverges sharply between Apache Software Foundation stewardship (ECharts), corporate-but-stable backing (Highcharts/Highsoft, Plotly), and visibly slowing single-maintainer or post-acquisition projects (Victory, visx, Frappe Charts, Chartist's original repo). Across nearly every library, three categories are broken by default rather than merely imperfect: accessibility (a 2026 ten-library audit found only two libraries ship any default accessible structure ([ApexCharts a11y audit](https://apexcharts.com/blog/charting-library-accessibility-audit/))), animation on data *updates* (as opposed to initial render, which is fine almost everywhere), and mobile touch interaction (tooltip-stuck-on-screen and off-viewport bugs recur nearly identically across Chart.js, Recharts, Highcharts, and ngx-charts). The practical consequence, visible across dozens of GitHub issues and vendor comparisons, is that production teams don't pick one chart library — they assemble a stack: a general-purpose library for standard charts, a specialized Canvas/WebGL library (uPlot, Lightweight Charts, LightningChart) for real-time or large-N data, and often a third niche package (d3-sankey-diagram, chartjs-chart-sankey, ApexGantt) for the exotic chart type their product actually needs. This fragmentation is the structural opportunity for a new general-purpose library: the gaps are well-known, well-documented, and consistent across completely unrelated codebases, which means they are architectural, not incidental, and therefore fixable by deliberate design rather than incremental patching.

## Rendering architecture is destiny: SVG, Canvas, and WebGL predetermine almost everything else

The most consequential decision any charting library makes is its rendering substrate, because that one choice cascades into performance ceiling, accessibility posture, and SSR compatibility as near-automatic consequences rather than independent engineering problems. The empirical ladder is consistent across sources: **SVG-based rendering tops out around 1,000–10,000 points** before frame rates degrade, **Canvas-based rendering** (Chart.js, uPlot, ECharts' default renderer) **comfortably handles tens of thousands to ~100,000 points**, and only **WebGL/WebGPU renderers** (Plotly's `scattergl`, deck.gl, LightningChart, the experimental ChartGPU) **sustain interactivity into the millions-of-points range** ([SVG Genie 2025](https://www.svggenie.com/blog/svg-vs-canvas-vs-webgl-performance-2025), [DEV: visualizing millions of points](https://dev.to/andyb1979/how-to-visualize-millions-of-data-points-efficiently-54ib)). Concrete numbers bear this out: a Scott Logic benchmark clocked deck.gl's WebGL path at **~79ms**, ECharts' optimized Canvas path at **~305ms**, and Plotly's WebGL mode at **~374ms** to render one million points, while plain D3-on-SVG requires full WebGL-shader reimplementation to even approach that scale ([Scott Logic](https://blog.scottlogic.com/2020/05/01/rendering-one-million-points-with-d3.html)). uPlot, a narrowly-scoped Canvas library, renders **166,650 points in 25ms** and streams **3,600 points at 60fps on just 10% CPU and 12.3MB RAM** — roughly a quarter the CPU and a sixth the memory of the next-fastest Canvas competitors, Chart.js and ECharts, on the same workload ([uPlot README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leeoniya/uPlot/master/README.md)). At the extreme commercial end, LightningChart JS claims GPU vertex-buffer streaming sustaining **24 million points/frame at 60 FPS** across 400 channels — a vendor claim, not independently verified, but directionally consistent with the WebGL tier's advantage ([LightningChart 2026 comparison](https://lightningchart.com/blog/the-ultimate-javascript-charting-library-comparison-2026/)).

This same architectural fork determines accessibility and SSR viability as a side effect, not a separate feature decision. SVG is markup — text and shapes sitting in the DOM — so accessibility hooks and server-rendering "come for free" in principle ([accessibility.build guide](https://accessibility.build/guides/accessible-charts)); Canvas is an opaque bitmap with "no internal structure to read," which is why Chart.js's own documentation places the entire burden of ARIA roles and fallback content on the developer, and why multi-year-old accessibility feature requests (#3970, #10450, #1976) remain open on that repo ([Chart.js Accessibility docs](https://www.chartjs.org/docs/latest/general/accessibility.html)). But SVG alone doesn't guarantee SSR safety either: Recharts and Plotly are SVG-based yet still fail to render meaningful markup server-side, because their *component architecture* — not their SVG output — depends on browser-only `window`/`document`/Context propagation via `useEffect` ([recharts/recharts#4336](https://github.com/recharts/recharts/issues/4336), [react-plotly.js#21](https://github.com/plotly/react-plotly.js/issues/21)). visx is the sharpest counter-example: because it's "a state and SVG geometry engine" with no DOM-context dependency, it renders meaningfully on React Server Components with zero configuration, while Observable Plot can SSR static SVG but sacrifices interactivity entirely, forcing a binary choice between a static image and a client-hydrated bundle ([Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46708095)). Apache ECharts only closed this gap for Canvas-rendering libraries relatively recently, adding a dedicated zero-dependency server-side SVG rendering mode in v5.3.0 ([ECharts SSR handbook](https://apache.github.io/echarts-handbook/en/how-to/cross-platform/server/)) — evidence that SSR-safety was bolted on after the fact rather than designed in. Bundle size tells the same architectural story from a different angle: lean, narrowly-scoped libraries (visx core packages at ~5KB gzipped each, uPlot at ~23KB gzip, Chartist at ~11.5KB) sit an order of magnitude below "batteries-included" libraries that ship many chart types and interaction features by default (ECharts ~359KB gzip full build, Plotly.js ~3.6MB minified, with real-world React bundles reported exceeding 10MB before manual partial-bundle configuration) ([Plotly community forum](https://community.plotly.com/t/plotly-js-size-is-huge-3mb-in-production-build/45407), [ApexCharts: State of JS Charting 2026](https://apexcharts.com/blog/state-of-javascript-charting-2026/)). The unifying lesson: a library's rendering substrate is not one property among many — it is the hidden variable that silently sets the ceiling on four or five "unrelated" dimensions at once, which means a new library's single most important early decision is not "which chart types" but "which renderer, and can it change per-chart-type without changing the API."

## Ease and depth sit on one brutal tradeoff curve, with no library escaping it

Every library in this landscape sits somewhere on a single ease-versus-control axis, and moving toward either end reliably produces the opposite end's complaint. At the control extreme, **D3.js** offers "unmatched flexibility for bespoke visualizations" and is the substrate nearly everything else compiles toward or wraps ([LightningChart 2026](https://lightningchart.com/blog/the-ultimate-javascript-charting-library-comparison-2026/)), but its enter/update/exit/`.join()` data-binding pattern is described by its own community as making code "inaccessible AF" for newcomers and prone to silent failure — "without `.merge()` in precisely the right spot... everything breaks with no warning" ([HN "Why is D3 so Verbose?"](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44970981), [HN "The Trouble with D3"](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=17298072)). A production D3 bar chart with axes and scales commonly runs **40–60+ lines**, versus a one-line call in **Observable Plot** or an **~18-line declarative spec** in **Vega-Lite** ([FreeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/how-to-create-your-first-bar-chart-with-d3-js-a0e8ea2df386/), [Vega-Lite bar example](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/examples/bar.html)). Plot and Vega-Lite buy that conciseness by constraining data shape and, in Plot's specific case, by shipping **no built-in animation and only weak interactivity** (no native tooltips with HTML/styling, no brushing, no pan/zoom) — the single most consistent complaint about Plot across its own review ecosystem ([vis4.net review](https://www.vis4.net/blog/2023/09/observable-plot-review/)). Vega-Lite goes further declaratively (layer/facet/concat/repeat composition, first-class interactive selections) but pays for it with the heaviest bundle in its category (~87KB vega-lite + ~182KB vega, min+gzip, typically shipped together) and a documented, counterintuitive performance regression where sampling 50K points *down* to 10 is slower than just drawing all 50K ([vega/vega-lite Discussion #7916](https://github.com/vega/vega-lite/discussions/7916)); once a chart needs anything outside the grammar, Vega-Lite's own docs say plainly "you will be writing Vega or dropping to D3" — an explicit, documented declarative ceiling unique among the libraries studied ([vega.github.io/vega-lite](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/)).

Mainstream "easy" libraries solve the verbosity problem but reproduce it one layer up, in the gap between a demo chart and a production one. Chart.js's eight built-in chart types and canvas-config-object API make it the default choice for simple dashboards (67.7k GitHub stars), but its plugin system registers globally across all chart instances regardless of per-chart configuration — a documented, counterintuitive behavior ([chartjs/Chart.js#7963](https://github.com/chartjs/Chart.js/issues/7963)) — and anything outside its eight types (Sankey, gauges) requires community plugins that lag each Chart.js major version. ApexCharts and Plotly represent the "batteries-included" response — most interactivity ships without opt-in wiring — but at real bundle cost (ApexCharts ~164KB gzip; Plotly's scientific-chart depth, including 3D/contour/violin/choropleth types, comes at the ~3.6MB price documented above). The React ecosystem shows the same curve internally: Recharts (27.6k stars, the most popular pure-React option) is praised for pre-built, well-documented charts but carries a long, well-documented history of re-render and large-dataset performance problems — deep-equal prop comparisons, anonymous event handlers forcing full re-renders, multi-second renders past ~10,000 points, and crashes on scatter plots past ~50,000 points ([recharts/recharts#1146](https://github.com/recharts/recharts/issues/1146), [Discussion #3181](https://github.com/recharts/recharts/discussions/3181)) — which Recharts 3 (July 2025) addressed partly via a state-management rewrite and default `accessibilityLayer`, without clear independent evidence the raw large-N ceiling moved ([Recharts v3.0.0 release notes](https://github.com/recharts/recharts/releases/tag/v3.0.0)). visx sits at the opposite pole from Recharts/Nivo/Victory inside the same ecosystem: it is explicitly "not a charting library" but low-level primitives — "you'll end up building your own charting library" — delivering bundle-size discipline (~30–40KB gzip for a typical custom chart assembled from several visx packages) and total creative control, at the cost of a steep learning curve that visibly deters developers without in-house D3/dataviz expertise ([Airbnb visx](https://airbnb.tech/opensource/visx/), [PkgPulse bundle comparison](https://www.pkgpulse.com/guides/recharts-vs-chartjs-vs-nivo-vs-visx-react-charting-2026)). Underlying almost all of this friction, especially in React, is a deeper architectural mismatch: imperative, DOM-owning libraries (D3 above all) conflict with a framework's declarative virtual-DOM cycle, forcing an explicit `useRef`/`useEffect` escape hatch whose cleanup logic is a well-known source of memory leaks and stale-DOM bugs ([react.dev useEffect reference](https://react.dev/reference/react/useEffect)); the entire category of "framework-native declarative" chart libraries (Recharts, Nivo, Victory, vue-chartjs) exists specifically so application developers never have to touch that escape hatch themselves. No library has found a way to be simultaneously D3-level flexible, config-light, and architecturally native to a reactive framework — every one of the 24 libraries studied picks at most two of those three properties.

## Licensing has quietly become a landmine as "open source" names monetize

A developer picking a charting library in 2026 purely from reputation risks a real governance surprise, because several widely-cited "open source" projects are commercial products with free tiers, not permissively-licensed OSS, and at least one has changed that status recently enough that older roundup posts are actively misleading. **ApexCharts** — still described in older comparison content as simply open source — is now "provided by Apex Software, Inc., a wholly owned subsidiary of Infragistics" under a dual Community/Commercial model: free only for organizations under **$2 million** in annual revenue/funding, with redistribution (bundling it into a resold SDK or dashboard builder) **not permitted** under either the Community or standard Commercial Developer tier without a separate paid OEM license ([ApexCharts Community License](https://apexcharts.com/license/community/), [ApexCharts OEM License](https://apexcharts.com/license/oem/)); GitHub's own license detector returns `NOASSERTION` for the repo, consistent with a non-standard license rather than plain MIT ([apexcharts/apexcharts.js](https://github.com/apexcharts/apexcharts.js)). **Highcharts** and **amCharts** are both source-viewable but require paid commercial licenses for essentially any company, client, or internal-business use, free only for education and registered nonprofits — Highcharts' three-tier Internal/SaaS/OEM structure creates classification friction beyond the ~$176–$366/developer/year price range cited across aggregators, and amCharts 5's official pricing page shows no visible free-with-watermark path, a tightening relative to amCharts 3/4's older "linkware" reputation ([Highcharts License Agreement](https://shop.highcharts.com/license-eula-1.0.pdf), [amCharts 5 pricing](https://www.amcharts.com/online-store/)). **LightningChart JS** is fully commercial with only a non-commercial, watermarked Community tier — there is no path to free commercial use at any data volume. **Google Charts** is a different kind of risk entirely: it is not an installable OSS package but a proprietary library loaded at runtime from Google's `gstatic.com` CDN, and Google's terms explicitly forbid self-hosting it offline — a dependency model with real precedent for failure, since Google's sibling Image Charts API was shut down outright in 2019 after being deprecated for seven years ([Google Charts FAQ](https://developers.google.com/chart/interactive/faq)). **Lightweight-charts** complicates the picture differently: it's genuinely Apache-2.0, but an unresolved GitHub Discussion shows a developer uncertain whether its `attributionLogo` option satisfies the license's mandatory-attribution requirement on every page view — a compliance ambiguity rather than a license restriction, but one that still demands legal review before commercial adoption ([Discussion #2109](https://github.com/tradingview/lightweight-charts/discussions/2109)).

Against this, a smaller set of libraries are genuinely, unambiguously permissive and tend to carry the strongest institutional backing: **Apache ECharts** (Apache-2.0, an Apache Software Foundation Top-Level Project originally donated by Baidu, ~67k stars) and **Plotly.js** (MIT, 258 GitHub contributors, Dash also MIT) sit furthest from any licensing risk, and **AntV G2/G2Plot** (MIT, backed by Ant Group/Alibaba's design team) is equally clean on paper even though its English-language mindshare trails ECharts sharply (a LibHunt popularity score of 5.5 with *declining* activity versus ECharts' 9.9 with *growing* activity) ([LibHunt: G2Plot vs ECharts](https://js.libhunt.com/compare-g2plot-vs-echarts)). Governance health varies independently of license terms: **Tremor**, acquired by Vercel in January 2025, had all its components (including "Blocks") made free and open source as part of the deal, with its founders joining Vercel's Design Engineering team — a meaningful de-risking versus its prior YC-backed-startup status, though Tremor's charts are themselves a styling layer over Recharts and inherit Recharts' underlying performance characteristics rather than solving them independently ([DeepNewz coverage](https://deepnewz.com/software/vercel-acquires-tremor-open-sources-all-react-components-charts-dashboards-now-84750143)). Several MIT-licensed, nominally-safe projects show real maintenance drift that a license check alone wouldn't surface: **Victory**'s core web package has slowed to roughly a push every few months following Formidable's rebrand into Nearform, with sub-projects like `victory-cli` and the standalone `victory-chart` repo explicitly deprecated or archived, even as Victory Native was separately rewritten onto Skia/Reanimated and remains energetically maintained ([FormidableLabs org page](https://github.com/FormidableLabs)); **visx** spent roughly seven months in alpha reaching its v4.0 stable release in June 2026, with its own community discussion documenting "extended periods without maintainer updates" and "heavy users" migrating away over reliability concerns ([GitHub Discussion #1908](https://github.com/airbnb/visx/discussions/1908)); and **Frappe Charts**, despite 15k+ stars, last tagged a release in **April 2022** and should be treated as semi-abandoned as a standalone package even though it still sees indirect use inside Frappe's own ERPNext ecosystem. The practical takeaway is that "MIT license" and "actively maintained by a resourced team" are independent variables that must both be checked — ECharts' ASF stewardship and Highcharts' 50+-person Highsoft team represent the most durable institutional bets in this survey, specifically because governance quality, not license text, is what predicts a library's five-year trajectory.

## Accessibility, touch interaction, and live-update animation are broken by default almost everywhere

Three categories of defect recur across libraries with completely unrelated codebases and maintainers, which is the strongest evidence available that these are industry-wide blind spots rather than isolated bugs. On accessibility, a 2026 audit of ten major libraries found that **only two — ApexCharts and Highcharts — ship a default ARIA role, accessible name, and SVG title/description out of the box**; six libraries, including D3, Chartist, Plotly.js, Chart.js, ECharts, and amCharts 5, **ship charts with zero accessible structure by default**, and — tellingly — automated accessibility scanners are structurally blind to the problem, since all ten libraries scored zero violations despite six shipping nothing accessible at all ([ApexCharts Accessibility Audit](https://apexcharts.com/blog/charting-library-accessibility-audit/)). This is a capability-versus-default gap, not a capability gap: ECharts has had WAI-ARIA support since v4, but it is off by default and requires manually importing a separate component since v5 ([ECharts accessibility handbook](https://apache.googlesource.com/echarts-handbook/+/4822ab31a3ba7257062b860e3652d1eefaef2bb8/contents/en/best-practices/aria.md)); the same "exists but opt-in" pattern recurs for lightweight-charts (an official but separate `@tradingview/lwc-plugin-accessibility` plugin) and Observable Plot (basic `ariaLabel`/`ariaDescription` support added reactively in response to a GitHub issue, not present from launch). Only **Highcharts** bundles a genuinely mature accessibility module — screen-reader point navigation, keyboard control, sonification — with every commercial license by default, and only **Google Charts** uniquely renders a data-table fallback automatically, described in the audit as "the single most robust pattern" found anywhere in the survey. The gap is severe enough that an entire category of toolkit-agnostic remediation libraries exists purely to patch it after the fact — Olli (keyboard-navigable structure) and AutoVizuA11y (automated accessible chart generation) both work *regardless* of which base charting library produced the chart, which is itself proof that no base library's native story is considered sufficient ([MIT Vis Group: Olli](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/olli), [feedzai/AutoVizuA11y](https://github.com/feedzai/AutoVizuA11y)).

Mobile touch handling shows the identical pattern of independently-discovered, nearly identical bugs: tooltips that remain stuck on screen after a finger lifts recur across Chart.js ([#7738](https://github.com/tradingview/lightweight-charts/issues/7738)), Recharts ([#444](https://github.com/recharts/recharts/issues/444)), Highcharts ([#5841](https://github.com/highcharts/highcharts/issues/5841)), and ngx-charts ([#511](https://github.com/swimlane/ngx-charts/issues/511)) — a pattern one analysis traces to a single root cause: "code written for a mouse and never exercised by a finger" ([plotset.com](https://plotset.com/blog/your-chart-breaks-on-the-phone-where-most-people-read-it)). The same source quantifies a viewport-clamping bug concretely: on a 375px-wide screen, a 270px tooltip that fails to clamp to viewport bounds flips to land **two-thirds off-screen** for any tap past the 52%-width mark. Only lightweight-charts and D3's `d3-zoom`/`d3-drag` modules ship genuinely first-class, default-on touch gestures (kinetic scroll, pinch-to-zoom); every other library in the survey treats touch as a secondary translation layer over mouse-event code, not a first-class input model designed in from the start. Animation shows a third, related failure mode: initial-render animation is solid nearly everywhere, but animating *data updates* is broken even in the two most mature libraries studied — ECharts line charts "fail to animate on options changes, updating immediately... like they do on initial load" ([apache/echarts#21542](https://github.com/apache/echarts/issues/21542)), and Chart.js users report that because "the entire chart is re-rendered" on data change, "seamless animation between changed values" is architecturally impossible without a true enter/update/exit transition model ([chartjs/Chart.js#5099](https://github.com/chartjs/Chart.js/issues/5099)). This is the direct cost of choosing a config-driven, full-re-render architecture over D3's data-join primitive — and it is precisely why real-time/streaming use cases abandon general-purpose libraries entirely in favor of purpose-built ones like uPlot and lightweight-charts, which sidestep the problem by eliminating animation rather than solving it ("no transitions or animations — they're always pure distractions," per uPlot's own README) ([uPlot README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leeoniya/uPlot/master/README.md)).

## Production teams assemble multi-library stacks because no single library covers the full matrix

The recurring real-world pattern across every research track is that teams do not standardize on one charting library — they combine two or three, each covering a gap the others leave open, and this pattern is strong enough to be read as a structural indictment of the current landscape rather than a one-off integration choice. The clearest version is the niche-chart-type split: heavy, feature-complete libraries (amCharts' 60+ types, Highcharts' 65, Nivo's 32, ECharts' broad catalog including Sankey/treemap/network/geo) bundle exotic chart types natively, while every lightweight general-purpose library (Chart.js core, Recharts core, ApexCharts core, visx core) ships none of them, spawning a visible satellite-package ecosystem — `chartjs-chart-sankey`, `d3-sankey-diagram`, and ApexCharts' own separately-branded ApexGantt/ApexSankey/ApexStock/ApexMaps/ApexTree products — to backfill the gap. That satellite ecosystem has its own churn risk: at least one such package, `d3.chart.sankey`, is already archived and unmaintained, meaning the "bolt-on" strategy trades feature completeness for a second, weaker maintenance-health bet. The same splitting logic governs performance: general-purpose libraries are optimized for "render once, update occasionally," while a distinct tier — uPlot, lightweight-charts, LightningChart — exists purely for the "redraw every few milliseconds forever" access pattern that financial tickers and IoT dashboards require; GitLab's own engineering blog, explaining its choice of ECharts, explicitly flags that ECharts' performance "does decrease as data points increase" starting around 4,000+ points in their testing, well below LightningChart's claimed WebGL ceiling ([GitLab: Why we chose ECharts](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/why-we-chose-echarts/)). Narrow financial libraries compound this: lightweight-charts is excellent for OHLC/candlestick time series but has **zero general chart grammar** — no pie, no categorical bar, no scatter, no maps — so any fintech product that also needs a portfolio-allocation pie chart is architecturally required to adopt a second library alongside it.

SSR support follows a comparable three-way split rather than a single fix: some teams simply disable SSR for chart components (`dynamic(..., { ssr: false })`, the most common workaround across Recharts, react-chartjs-2, and react-plotly.js integrations); SVG-native libraries with no DOM-context dependency (visx, and Observable Plot for static output) render meaningfully on the server but either sacrifice interactivity or require React Server Components discipline; and Canvas-based libraries need a dedicated server-rendering shim, which ECharts only shipped starting at v5.3.0 — meaning for most of its history, even one of the most popular libraries in the survey had no server-rendering story at all ([ECharts SSR handbook](https://apache.github.io/echarts-handbook/en/how-to/cross-platform/server/)). Maintenance health itself varies widely enough within this 24-library set that "is this library safe to depend on" cannot be answered from stars or license alone: Chart.js, Billboard.js, Unovis, and the community Chartist fork all show same-day commit activity as of this research, while Frappe Charts (last tagged release April 2022), the original `gionkunz/chartist-js` repo (explicitly relabeled "Legacy" by its own owner, down to 95 stars), and Victory's web package (quarterly-cadence pushes post-Nearform rebrand) sit in varying stages of de facto maintenance mode despite none being formally archived. Taken together, this fragmentation — one library for standard charts, a second for scale/streaming, a third for the one exotic chart type a product actually needs, and a workaround layer for SSR — is the single clearest signal in the entire landscape that demand exists for a library that closes these gaps in one coherent design rather than requiring an integration project to stitch three partial solutions together.

## Conclusion: what the gaps imply for a new general-purpose library

The research converges on a specific, actionable reading: the "best-in-class general-purpose" slot is open not because existing libraries are poorly built, but because each one made an early, hard-to-reverse architectural bet (SVG vs. Canvas, config-object vs. primitives, framework-native vs. D3-wrapped) that locks in a predictable set of strengths and an equally predictable set of structural weaknesses elsewhere. The clearest white space is a library that treats rendering substrate as a runtime decision rather than a founding commitment — rendering standard charts in accessible, SSR-safe SVG while transparently switching to Canvas or WebGL past a data-volume threshold under the same declarative API — because every one of the libraries studied that tried to be universally one substrate (all-SVG or all-Canvas) eventually hit the ceiling that forced users toward a second specialized tool. Equally actionable: accessibility, touch gestures, and true enter/update/exit animation semantics are inexpensive to build in from day one and devastatingly expensive to retrofit, as evidenced by the fact that even mature, well-funded projects (ECharts, Chart.js, Recharts) still carry multi-year-old open issues in exactly these three areas; a library that ships ARIA structure, data-table fallbacks, 24px-minimum touch targets, and genuine data-join animation as defaults rather than opt-ins would be addressing the single most consistently cited gap across this entire survey. Licensing and governance are no longer a secondary concern a technical evaluation can skip — the ApexCharts relicensing and the amCharts 5 tightening show that even projects with years of "open source" reputation can change the deal, which means a credible new entrant should commit explicitly and durably to a permissive license (MIT/Apache-2.0) and transparent, foundation-style or multi-maintainer governance rather than a single-vendor dual-license structure, precisely to be the stable, low-risk long-term bet that ECharts' ASF status and Highcharts' Highsoft backing currently represent at opposite ends of the licensing spectrum.
